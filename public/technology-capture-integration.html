01

Technology

Gaussian splatting is the reason photoreal 3D suddenly runs in an ordinary browser tab. It is worth a few paragraphs, because how it works explains both why it looks the way it does and where it has honest limits.

What a splat actually is

A conventional 3D model is built from polygons, typically flat triangles with a photograph stretched over them. A Gaussian splat is built from millions of soft, translucent, lens-shaped blobs. Each blob stores a position, a size and orientation, an opacity, and a color that changes depending on the viewing angle.

That last property is what makes 3DGS perfect for photorealistic recreations. A lacquered panel, a polished 1176 faceplate, the sheen along a speaker cone: those surfaces do not have one color, they have a colour per viewing angle. A splat can accurately recreate that.

A scan is grown from points then corrected

The animation above is close to literal. A splat scene is not assembled from your photographs; it is repeatedly corrected against them.

  1. Seed. A rough cloud of points recovered from a camera solve. Every point becomes a blob. It looks like a typical pointcloud.
  2. Train. The renderer draws what the current cloud would look like from camera positions.
  3. Compare. That guess is measured against the actual photograph taken from that position.
  4. Assign. Because the renderer is differentiable, errors can be traced back to which blobs caused it. Each one is nudged a fraction closer to the solve.
  5. Densify. Where error stays stubborn, blobs split to add resolution; blobs contributing nothing are deleted.

This process then repeats tens of thousands of times, across every photograph at once. The best way to think about it is like this: the photographs are an answer key for the gaussians to train against. The optimiser is hunting for one arrangement of blobs that satisfies every photograph simultaneously. Anything that makes two photographs disagree about the same surface (a light that shifted, a soft frame, a reflection that slid across a panel) is a contradiction it must resolve, and it usually resolves it by inventing geometry that was never there. An accurate and controlled capture reduces these contradictions, but may not eliminate them entirely.

How it differs from Photogrammetry and LiDAR

Photogrammetry

Solves for
A polygon mesh with a flat photographic texture baked onto it.
Strong at
Hard, matte, evenly lit geometry. Produces a measurable model that drops into CAD.
Struggles with
One colour per point of surface, so sheen and reflection bake in like paint. Thin objects — mic stands, cable runs, grille cloth — go blobby or vanish. Heavy file size because of resolution

LiDAR

Solves for
Geometry directly. It fires a laser and times the return, which creates an accurate measurement of geometry.
Strong at
Darkness, featureless surfaces, and dimensional accuracy.
Struggles with
Appearance is captured separately. Color comes from a small panoramic camera spreading its resolution across a whole sphere. The output is a point cloud, not a space.

Gaussian splatting

Solves for
An appearance model. Millions of view-dependent blobs are fitted to photographs.
Strong at
Photorealism including soft reflection and real depth, rendered in real time in browsers with small filesize. Fidelity holds equally in a booth and in a large live room.
Struggles with
Survey grade measurements. Reflective and screen-lit surfaces can create artifacts. A capture is a fixed moment; rearranging a room means re-shooting it.

These should be thought of as different instruments used for different goals. LiDAR is the right answer when you need dimensions. Photogrammetry is the right answer when you need a mesh to build on for videogames. Splatting is the right answer when the goal is for someone to feel like they are standing in a lifelike room, which directly benefits this usecase the most. They also combine: a LiDAR pass makes a sound geometry backbone underneath a high-fidelity photographic capture, and that is the natural direction for this pipeline as the spaces get larger.

02

Capture

Each room is shot as one continuous scan rather than a set of separate passes stitched together afterwards. Two kinds of pass go into it, and both are shown below.

The room pass

Three loops around the room's outer perimeter, at different heights but similar distances from the walls. This allows for consistent viewing of a space despite viewing angle, position and direction.

  • Walking pace is tied to subject distance. Motion blur scales with speed divided by distance, so the wide loops move at roughly twice the pace of the close work.
  • Nothing is on automatic. Shutter, aperture, focus, ISO and white balance are set once and left for the entire session. Every automatic adjustment is a mechanism for making the same surface look different between two frames, which is exactly the contradiction the optimiser cannot resolve.
  • The take can be split, the coverage cannot. Recording can stop and restart — from the same spot, at the same framing — without harming the result. What must stay unbroken is the chain of overlapping views.

The Hero Pass

The room loops cannot deliver a console faceplate or the top of a synth — they are too far away and at the wrong angle. Featured gear gets its own passes, shot inside the same continuous take so there is no compositing and no risk of scale drift between the close-up and the room around it.

Three arcs across the front, at low, eye and high angles, holding a constant distance so the object stays at one scale throughout. The arcs stop rather than close, and that is deliberate on two counts. A visitor will never see the back of a monitor bolted into a soffit, and more importantly, training averages error across all frames roughly evenly — a surplus of low-value views will outvote the detailed ones on every surface they share. Frames are an allotted budget for the compiler, and they get spent on the angles someone will actually look at.

The dot spacing in the second panel is tighter than in the first on purpose: a hero pass samples several times more densely per foot travelled than a room pass does.

What the room needs for capture day

  • The space presented the way you want it remembered: gear placed, cables dressed, and surfaces tidy.
  • Lighting set even, neutral and bright, then left alone for the whole session. This is the single largest quality lever, and your control over it is a real technical advantage.
  • The room clear of movement and people while filming. Anything that moves becomes a smear.
  • Roughly 60 to 90 minutes per room, depending on how many hero pieces it carries.
  • mood lighting is applied afterwards as a color grade to frames that recorded the detail. Bright and even lighting on capture day is optimal; atmosphere can be added later.
03

Integration

A capture that runs on any modern machine is the goal with these captures, so it is worth being specific about how the scans are shown and about how the viewer integrates seamlessly into your existing webpage.

Why the graphics API matters here

Splats are translucent, and translucency is order-dependent: to blend correctly they have to be drawn back to front. That means re-sorting millions of blobs by distance every time the camera moves. One requirement, and it is the requirement that separates the two available graphics paths in a browser.

WebGPU exposes compute shaders — general-purpose programs that run on the graphics card. The depth sort runs on the GPU in parallel, every frame, alongside the rendering. Ordering is always correct and the blob budget is high. PlayCanvas, the engine underneath your viewer, has one of the more mature compute-based splat renderers on the web.

WebGL2, the older path, has no compute shaders at all. The sort has to run on the CPU, normally in a background worker, and it cannot keep up with the frame rate. In practice the picture renders at full speed while the ordering trails a few frames behind: invisible when panning gently, briefly visible as a shimmer when the camera swings hard. The blob budget is lower too, because both the sort and the cost of uploading a new order scale with count.

This is a materially different picture from even a year ago. Until Safari 26, iPhone was the one mainstream platform with no compute path — and because Apple requires every iOS browser to use its own engine, "open it in Chrome instead" was not a workaround. Safari 26 closed that gap, and WebGPU is now on by default in all four major browser engines. The fallback still earns its place, for older iOS versions, Android hardware without Vulkan, Firefox on Linux, and locked-down corporate machines. WebGL2 It is now the exception rather than the mobile default.

The ladder, and what a visitor sees at each rung

  • WebGPU available. Full scene, full blob count, correct ordering, smooth guided fly-ins. What most desktop and current-generation phone visitors will get.
  • WebGL2 only. The same scene and the same interactions at a reduced blob count, with sorting moved to a worker. Visibly the same experience; marginally softer, and occasionally shimmering on a hard camera swing.
  • Neither. A still poster frame of the room and a plain explanation — never a blank canvas or a broken page. Detected before anything loads, not after it fails.

Worth noting alongside that: nothing graphics-related initialises until a visitor actually clicks into a viewer. Someone who reads your studio page and never opens a room pays none of the loading cost. That is a deliberate choice, and it is why an interactive 3D feature does not impede the page's performance.

How the viewer becomes yours

A third-party embed announces whose platform it is: someone else's styling, someone else's typography, a watermark, and a page that lives on their domain. The alternative is a viewer built as part of your site, and the difference shows in five specific places.

  • It sits in the page, not over it. The 3D room is a window set into your layout, with your headings and your copy around it, not a full-screen takeover a visitor has to open separately from. The panels above are that pattern in miniature.
  • Everything visual comes from a short list of tokens. Paper, ink, one accent, two typefaces. Re-theming the entire surface is editing that list rather than rebuilding the interface — this page and the viewer both work that way.
  • Hero points are data, not code. Each one is a row: a label, a short note, a camera position, an anchor in 3D space. Adding a piece of gear means adding a row; the menu, the marker and the guided fly-in all follow from it. Which means the content can change without touching the build.
  • One room in memory at a time. Moving between studios loads the new scene and frees the old one. Unglamorous, and it is the thing that keeps a five-room facility viable on a phone.
  • It ships as static files on your own domain. HTML, JavaScript and compressed scene data — no platform account, no per-space hosting fee, no third-party roadmap that can deprecate it. You also get a private standalone link for pitches, which is the same build served from a different address.

The honest costs

  • A room is tens of megabytes. The first open shows a brief loading state; larger rooms are larger downloads, and the Common Room will be the biggest of them.
  • Older phones will run at reduced blob counts and lower frame rates. Usable but not identical.
  • Scenes stream one at a time, which is what keeps memory in check but does mean a short wait when moving from one studio to another.
